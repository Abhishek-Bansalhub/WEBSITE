SARFAESI proceedings: mapping the borrower's remedies

A practical framework for understanding statutory remedies, limitation and the role of the DRT.

**A Practical Framework for Understanding Statutory Remedies, Limitation, and the Role of the Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT)** In India’s banking and financial sector, the recovery of non-performing assets has long posed systemic challenges. Traditional civil court litigation proved slow and ineffective for banks and financial institutions. To address this, Parliament created specialised statutory mechanisms under the Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993 (RDB Act, formerly the Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act) and the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 (SARFAESI Act). At the centre of this architecture stands the Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT). A practical understanding of statutory remedies, the operation of limitation periods, and the DRT’s dual role is essential for banks, borrowers, guarantors, and practitioners. ### The Statutory Architecture and Available Remedies Two primary statutory routes operate concurrently and complementarily. Under the RDB Act, banks and financial institutions may file an Original Application (OA) under Section 19 before the DRT for recovery of debts of ₹20 lakh or more (the current threshold under Section 1(4)). The DRT adjudicates the claim after examining loan documents, statements of account, and defences. Upon allowing the OA, it issues a Recovery Certificate (RC) under Section 19(22), which has the force of a civil court decree. The Recovery Officer then executes the RC through attachment, sale of assets, appointment of receivers, or other modes listed in Section 25. The SARFAESI Act provides a parallel, creditor-driven enforcement mechanism for secured debts. After classification of an account as a non-performing asset, the secured creditor issues a demand notice under Section 13(2). If the borrower fails to discharge the liability within 60 days, the creditor may take measures under Section 13(4), including possession of the secured asset (symbolic or physical), management of the asset, or sale. Physical possession often requires an order from the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or District Magistrate under Section 14. Sale proceeds are applied toward the debt; any shortfall may be recovered by filing an application under Section 13(10) before the DRT or a competent court. These remedies are not mutually exclusive. The Supreme Court in *Transcore v. Union of India* (2008) held that the doctrine of election does not apply; banks may pursue both routes simultaneously. Section 37 of the SARFAESI Act expressly states that its provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, the RDB Act. For borrowers and other aggrieved persons, the principal statutory remedy is an application under Section 17 of the SARFAESI Act before the DRT challenging measures taken under Section 13(4). No pre-deposit is required at the DRT stage. An appeal lies to the Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunal (DRAT) under Section 18, subject to a mandatory pre-deposit of 50 per cent of the debt (reducible to not less than 25 per cent). Section 34 of the SARFAESI Act bars the jurisdiction of civil courts in matters that the DRT or DRAT is empowered to decide, reinforcing the exclusivity of the statutory forums (subject to the limited residual jurisdiction of High Courts under Article 226 in exceptional cases of patent illegality or natural justice violations). ### Limitation: The Critical Time Framework Limitation operates differently depending on the nature of the proceeding, and Section 24 of the RDB Act plays a pivotal role by providing that the provisions of the Limitation Act, 1963, shall, as far as may be, apply to an application made to a Tribunal. For an Original Application under Section 19 of the RDB Act, the ordinary rules of the Limitation Act apply. In most cases involving simple money claims, the period is three years from the date the debt becomes due or the cause of action arises (Article 137 or related articles). Where the debt is secured by a mortgage, a longer period of twelve years may apply for enforcement of the security. Limitation can be extended by a written acknowledgment of debt under Section 18 or by part-payment meeting the requirements of Section 19 of the Limitation Act. Banks routinely obtain balance confirmation letters or revival letters to keep claims alive. Once an RC is issued, it is treated as a decree for execution purposes and remains enforceable for twelve years under Article 136. For applications under Section 17 of the SARFAESI Act, the statute itself prescribes a strict period of 45 days from the date of the measure taken under Section 13(4). Judicial interpretation has clarified that the Limitation Act applies to these applications as well. Through the combined operation of Section 17(7) of the SARFAESI Act (which requires disposal in accordance with the RDB Act) and Section 24 of the RDB Act, Sections 4 to 24 of the Limitation Act are attracted. Consequently, the DRT has the power to condone delay under Section 5 of the Limitation Act upon demonstration of sufficient cause. High Courts, including the Punjab and Haryana High Court and the Gauhati High Court, have affirmed this position, ensuring that the remedy is not rendered illusory by rigid timelines while still discouraging unexplained delay. Appeals to the DRAT under Section 18 of the SARFAESI Act must be filed within 30 days (extendable), subject to the pre-deposit condition. Appeals against orders of the Recovery Officer under Section 30 of the RDB Act are governed by a stricter 30-day period, and the Supreme Court has held that Section 5 of the Limitation Act does not apply to such appeals because Section 24 is confined to original applications under Section 19. In the parallel context of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, limitation for Section 7 applications is generally three years from the date of default (Article 137). Time spent in valid DRT or SARFAESI proceedings does not ordinarily exclude the period under Section 14 of the Limitation Act unless those proceedings suffered from a defect of jurisdiction. A Recovery Certificate itself can give rise to a fresh cause of action, allowing an IBC application within three years of its issuance. ### The Central Role of the DRT The DRT performs a dual function that forms the practical core of the framework. First, it is the adjudicatory forum for banks’ recovery claims under the RDB Act, providing a specialised, time-bound alternative to civil courts (with a statutory expectation of disposal within six months, though practice often exceeds this). Second, it acts as a check on the unilateral powers of secured creditors under SARFAESI by entertaining Section 17 applications. In both capacities, it exercises powers of a civil court for certain purposes under Section 22 of the RDB Act, including summoning witnesses, discovery, and interim relief. This dual role ensures balance. Banks obtain an efficient recovery mechanism and a decree-like instrument (the RC). Borrowers obtain a specialised forum to challenge procedural irregularities, valuation issues, or excesses in enforcement without the need for ordinary civil suits. The Recovery Officer’s execution powers further streamline the process once a certificate is issued. ### Practical Framework for Stakeholders A workable approach requires parties to map their position against three axes: the nature of the claim (secured or unsecured), the stage of enforcement, and the applicable limitation clock. Banks should maintain contemporaneous records of acknowledgments, issue compliant Section 13(2) notices, and file OAs or pursue SARFAESI measures within limitation. They may run parallel tracks but must avoid procedural defects that invite successful Section 17 challenges. Borrowers and guarantors must act promptly. The 45-day window under Section 17 is short; delay applications require credible explanation. In defending an OA, limitation remains a potent preliminary objection. Settlement through One-Time Settlement schemes remains available at almost every stage. Courts and tribunals consistently emphasise that statutory remedies must be exhausted before extraordinary writ jurisdiction is invoked. At the same time, the application of the Limitation Act to Section 17 proceedings prevents technical bars from defeating substantive justice where sufficient cause exists. In sum, the framework is coherent once the complementary nature of the RDB Act and SARFAESI Act, the specific limitation regimes, and the DRT’s dual adjudicatory and supervisory functions are clearly understood. Mastery of these elements enables efficient recovery for lenders and meaningful protection for borrowers within a specialised statutory ecosystem designed to balance commercial efficacy with fairness.
